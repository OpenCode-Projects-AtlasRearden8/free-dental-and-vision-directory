Hearing HelpHearing aids

Hearing aids can cost thousands of dollars per pair, and Medicare still doesn't cover them — which is why assistance charities exist. Here are the dependable doors, plus two low-cost routes most people never hear about.

Education, not medical advice. We list programs; we don't tell you which device or cause fits your ears. Rules change often — confirm with the program.

Charities that help pay for hearing aids

  • Hearing Loss Association of America (HLAA) https://www.hlaa.org The biggest consumer organization for hearing loss. Their “Get Started” resources explain all the assistance routes in one place, and local HLAA chapters are warm, knowledgeable, and free to attend.
  • Miracle-Ear Foundation https://www.miracle-ear.com/miracle-ear-foundation Provides donated Miracle-Ear hearing aids and services to income-eligible people with hearing loss that isn't covered by insurance. Apply through their site or a Miracle-Ear store; a referral professional usually signs off.
  • Starkey Hearing Foundation https://www.starkeyhearingfoundation.org Fits donated hearing aids worldwide and runs U.S.-based assistance programs (their “Hear Now” program historically serves low-income U.S. residents). Check the current program status on their site.
  • Lions Clubs International https://www.lionsclubs.org Lions run local hearing-assistance funds and hearing-aid recycling. Your town's Lions club is often the fastest answer: call and ask, “Do you help residents afford hearing aids or exams?”
  • Sertoma https://www.sertoma.org A service club focused on hearing health; historically funds community hearing-health projects and provides grants for assistive listening needs through local clubs.
  • 211 information line https://www.211.org Call 211 and ask for “hearing-aid assistance programs near me.” Some counties have discreet senior-service funds that make small grants for exactly this.

The two low-cost routes most people don't know

  • University audiology clinics. Every accredited audiology program trains graduate students in supervised clinics: full hearing tests and hearing-aid fittings at far lower prices than private offices. Search “accredited audiology programs” through the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association site (asha.org) to find the nearest university clinic, then ask for their patient clinic.
  • Over-the-counter hearing aids (OTC). Since 2022 U.S. rules allow hearing aids without a prescription for adults with mild-to-moderate perceived hearing loss. These are sold directly to consumers for hundreds, not thousands, of dollars. HLAA's site offers plain guidance on what OTC devices can and can't do.

Refurbished and donated devices

  • Hearing-aid recycling programs (Lions clubs, some manufacturer programs) collect donated used hearing aids, refurbish them, and fit them to people who qualify. Ask HLAA or your local Lions club which program is currently active near you — programs rotate.
  • State assistive-technology (AT) programs. Every U.S. state has a federally funded AT program that loans, demonstrates, and helps pay for assistive devices. Find yours via the AbilityTech directory: at3center.net (the AT3 center lists every state program). These programs are staffed by people whose whole job is “help you get the device you need.”
  • Medicaid: many states cover hearing aids for adults through Medicaid, and all cover children's hearing health. Check medicaid.gov for your state.

If hearing drops suddenly

Safety note (education, not advice): sudden or rapidly worsening hearing loss, hearing loss after a loud blast or illness, hearing loss with dizziness so bad you can't stand, or hearing loss with drainage — those need prompt medical evaluation, not a charity application. Programs above are for long-standing hearing needs.

Typical charity application flow

  1. Get an audiogram first. Almost every program requires a recent hearing test from an audiologist. University clinics are the budget route.
  2. Line up a referral professional. Like vision vouchers, many programs want a social worker, audiologist, or agency professional to co-sign. 211 can find your referral partner.
  3. Gather income proof. See what to bring.
  4. Ask what's included. Good questions: does the award include batteries or repairs? Which hearing-aid brand? How long does the process take?
